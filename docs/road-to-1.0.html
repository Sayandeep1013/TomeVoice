0.5.1 + 0.6 Ready to share

Make it safe to hand out

Android knows an app by its id. Ours is still app.tomevoice.tomevoice_spike. Changing it later makes everyone you shared with reinstall and lose their library, so it changes now, while that is you and a few friends. To make the change painless, backup has to exist first.

Backup and restore (0.5.1, under the old id)

  • Settings → Library → Back up library. Android's own save dialog picks where it goes (Downloads, Drive). The file is TomeVoice-backup-2026-10-24.zip.
  • What's inside: a manifest.json (backup format, app version, date, counts), library.json, prefs.json, updates.json, your installed sources and source lists (not site cookies), and for every book its file, its text, your place, covers and downloaded chapters. Later also your saved quotes and stats.
  • Size: your library is 92 MB on the phone, so a full backup is about that. A second choice, Places and settings only, makes a small file: web novels fetch their chapters again, but local books need their files.
  • How it's written: Kotlin streams the zip straight into the file Android hands us (java.util.zip), so even a 1 GB library never sits in memory, with a progress bar.
  • Restore: pick the zip. We read the manifest first, then ask: Replace my library, or Add to my library (adds missing books; for a book in both, keeps whichever place is further).

Moving to the new id (0.6)

  1. 1On the old app (0.5.1), tap Back up library. Save to Downloads.
  2. 2Install 0.6. Android treats it as a new app, so it starts fresh with the starter shelf. Your phone finally tests that shelf.
  3. 3On first launch it finds the backup in Downloads and offers Restore your library?
  4. 4Check your books and places, then uninstall the old app.

The id becomes one line in tools/prepare_android.py (applicationId). The Kotlin package name stays as it is: nobody sees it, and renaming it only moves files. The data folder moves to Android/data/app.tomevoice.

Also in 0.6

  • Telling people about updates. Once a day the app asks GitHub for the latest release (one request, no account). When there's a newer one, a quiet card on the library says TomeVoice 0.7 is out · What's new and opens the release page. It can be turned off in Settings.
  • An install guide for friends in the README and on each release: allow installs from the browser; if Play Protect calls it an unknown app, tap More details → Install anyway.
  • The signing key. Every update must be signed with the same key, which lives only as a GitHub secret. If it's ever lost, nobody can update; they would have to uninstall. Keep the .p12 file and its password somewhere offline as well, such as a password manager.
Your callThe APK is built for 64-bit phones only (arm64). Nearly every phone since 2019 is 64-bit, but some budget Android Go phones aren't, and on those the install fails with App not installed. A second APK for older phones is possible if the QuickJS engine builds for them (a CI check). Worth it?
0.7 Colour

Each book in its own colours

The orange stays only where no book is involved. A book's page, its reader and its player take the colour of its cover, the way the media card already does.

How the colour is picked

  1. 1Shrink the cover to 112 px, once per book, on a background isolate.
  2. 2Group its pixels into at most 128 colours with Material's quantizer, the same code Android uses for wallpaper colours. It already ships inside Flutter (material_color_utilities), so nothing new is added.
  3. 3Score them for how well each would work as an accent (colourful and common enough) and take the winner.
  4. 4Make tones from its hue: a light tone on dark grounds and a deep one on light grounds, so text on it always passes contrast.
  5. 5Store it on the book as tint in library.json, and redo it when the cover changes. A grey cover keeps the theme's accent.

0.7 Colour

Now Playing

Swipe up on the player bar, or tap its cover, and the book fills the screen. Swipe down to go back. The background is the cover, blurred and tinted with the book's colour. Under the title, the words scroll like lyrics: the sentence before, dim; the one being read, large, with the word under the voice lit; the next one, dim.

Built from pieces that already exist: the scheduler already reports the current sentence and word (WordMark), and the player bar already knows the time left. New: one screen. The blur is cheap because it blurs the 112 px cover from the colour step and scales it up.

0.8 Find and keep

Search, in the book and in the chapter

One search box, two scopes. In the reader, the search icon searches this chapter first, and one tap on Whole book widens it. From the book's page, search starts on the whole book.

  • How: the book's whole text is already on the phone, in the reading layout (every sentence). Search runs on a background isolate so typing never stutters. It ignores case and accents (cafe finds café), and whole words is an option.
  • Results are grouped by chapter, with a line of context for each and a count per chapter. Tap one and the reader jumps there with the matches marked; ↑ ↓ step through them, and ▶ reads from the match.
  • Web novels: only chapters on the phone can be searched, and the results say so: Searched 120 of 851 chapters · Download the rest.
0.8 Find and keep

Saved: bookmarks and quotes

A section of its own in the menu, Saved, beside History. Two tabs, Quotes and Bookmarks, grouped by book with its cover, with search. Tap one to open the reader there; swipe to delete. Each book's page also shows Saved in this book (4).

Three ways to save

  1. ReadingLong-press a sentence: Save quote · Bookmark · Copy · Share as picture · Fix pronunciation. A quote can take in the sentences before or after with + buttons.
  2. ListeningA bookmark button on the player bar saves the sentence being spoken.
  3. Lock screenThe media card has room for a second custom button beside speed, which becomes Save. Hear a line you love in bed, tap once.

What's stored, and keeping it in place

One small file, marks.json, at the library root, and part of the backup. Each entry holds:

  • the book
  • whether it's a quote or a bookmark
  • its place: chapter · paragraph · sentence, the same address as your reading place
  • how many sentences it covers
  • a copy of the text, so it still shows if the book is removed
  • the chapter title, an optional note, and the date

Local books are re-parsed when the reader improves. The code already carries your reading place across that, and saved places use the same carry-over. The copied text is a check: if the address no longer holds that text, it's found nearby.

Share as a picture

Flutter draws the card off-screen in the app's own fonts at 1080 × 1350 (a 4:5 post) or 1080 × 1920 (a story), saves it to a temporary file, and hands it to Android's share sheet through a FileProvider. prepare_android.py adds the FileProvider to the manifest. The background is the book's colour, deepened. On it: the quote in Newsreader, the title in Bodoni, the author, a small cover, and the TomeVoice mark.

0.8 Find and keep

Pronunciation fixes and cleaner chapters

These are two different tools. One changes what the voice is told to say, never the page. The other decides which lines are read at all.

Say this as that

On the pageZorian looked at Kael.
Your rulesZorian → Zor-ee-an
Kael → Kale
Sent to the voiceZor-ee-an looked at Kale.
Back from the voiceAudio, plus where each word starts in the text it was given
Mapped backZorian looked at Kael.
The highlight lands on the page's words
  • A rule is a whole word, any case, by default. It applies to one book or to every book. A pattern mode is there for odd cases.
  • To make one: long-press the word, tap Fix pronunciation, type how it sounds, tap ▶ to hear it, then Save. All rules are listed on the book's page and in Settings.
  • In code: one function between the sentence and the synthesise call in scheduler.dart. It returns the new text and a map of how positions moved; the word timings go through that map.

Which lines are read: the filter

There's already a filter (isWebJunk in web.dart) that works one paragraph at a time. It drops a paragraph only when the whole line looks like site furniture: Read the latest chapters at …, translator credits, Previous / Next links, rows of dashes. It is narrow on purpose, so a sentence that merely mentions a website stays. Two additions:

  • The repeated title. When a chapter's first line matches its title once Chapter, numbers, leading zeros and punctuation are ignored (44. A Show of Trust = Chapter 044 A Show of Trust), it's dropped.
  • Your own rules. Long-press a paragraph, then Don't read lines like this. Choose just this line, or every line containing words you pick, for this novel's site or everywhere. Rules apply when chapters are built, so chapters already downloaded clean up the next time the book opens.
0.9 Habits and home

Listening stats and streaks

What's counted

  • Listening time: only while words are actually playing, not paused or loading.
  • Reading time: the reader is open, nothing is playing, and you scrolled in the last minute.
  • Words heard, from the word counts the reader already keeps per sentence.
  • Chapters finished: listening or reading crossed a chapter's end.
  • Time per book.

How it's recorded

A small clock in the reader starts on play and stops on pause, stop or leaving. It writes to stats.json every 30 seconds, so if Android kills the app at most 30 seconds are lost, and at every stop. Totals are kept per day: listening, reading, words, chapters, and time per book. A day starts at 4 am, so listening at 1 am counts toward the night before.

Streaks

A day counts when you listen or read for at least 5 minutes. Today doesn't break the streak until today is over. The Stats screen (in the menu) shows the streak, this week by day, a year of dots (one per day, brighter for more), time per book and totals. It all stays on the phone and goes into the backup.

Your callThe daily minimum for a streak: 5, 10 or 15 minutes?
0.9 Habits and home

The home-screen widget

From Nothing: black surfaces, a dot-matrix face for numbers (Doto, an open-licence font, nothing proprietary), dots for progress, and circles. From TomeVoice: the cover, the title in Bodoni Moda, and the book's colour on the lit dots and the play button. It turns white when the phone is in light mode.

The streak widget

The same dot language as the player widget, but about the habit. The small one shows the streak, the week as dots, and today as a ring that closes when you reach the 5-minute goal. The wide one adds the last 18 weeks. Tap either to open Stats. The widget reads stats.json itself, so it updates when the app saves your time. A quiet refresh every 30 minutes, plus one at 4 am, rolls the day over.

Adding it

Long-press the home screen, then Widgets → TomeVoice, and pick a size. Then choose Continue listening, which follows whatever you read last, or a specific book from a list with covers. Tap the cover to open the book; tap play to play.

How play works

  • If TomeVoice is already reading or paused, play, pause and next go straight to the reading service. No app opens.
  • If it isn't, play opens TomeVoice straight into the reader and starts reading; lock the phone and it carries on. Reading runs inside the app's Flutter engine. Playing with no screen at all needs a background engine, which is possible later but a much bigger change.

Built natively in Kotlin (AppWidgetProvider and RemoteViews, no new libraries). The reading service refreshes it on every chapter or play change, and the app does when your place is saved. Android widgets can't reliably load custom fonts, so the dot numbers and dot progress are drawn into a bitmap; the title and chapter are plain text.

1.0 Voices

Neural voices: heavier and laggier?

The app gets a little bigger. It won't lag, because voices aren't part of the app; they're optional downloads. Battery use goes up while one reads. These are estimates until we measure on your phone, which is the first step.

QuestionAnswer
App sizeAbout +15–20 MB for the engine (sherpa-onnx with ONNX Runtime). An estimate, to be measured. Today's APK is 18 MB.
VoicesDownloaded only if you want one, one at a time. Piper voices are 20–130 MB each (about 63 MB for good quality). Kokoro, the best-sounding, is 90–330 MB.
MemoryPiper uses about 50–150 MB while reading. Kokoro uses several hundred MB, so it's offered only on phones with room to spare.
Lag on screenNone expected. Voices are made on a background thread, and the player already keeps about 10 s of audio ready ahead, so the screen never waits for the voice.
Falling behindPiper makes speech several times faster than it plays on a mid-range phone. Kokoro is around 0.45 (4.5 s to make 10 s): fine with the buffer, tight on slow phones. The app measures this on first use and warns if the phone can't keep up.
Word highlightThese engines don't say when each word starts. The sentence is always highlighted; the word is estimated from the pauses in the audio, which our pipeline already finds. That estimate needs testing.
BatteryMore than the phone's own voice. Measured in the trial.

The phone's voices stay the default and cost nothing. A neural voice is an opt-in download in the voice picker: Download a better voice · 63 MB, with a sample first. Before any of this is built, one Piper voice runs on your phone for 30 minutes to measure speed, memory and battery. If the numbers are bad, we stop there.

Measured on your phone (Alice at 2×, one minute each): the Google voice uses about 20% of one core and about 404 MB across the app and Google's speech app; Kristin uses 70% on average, in bursts up to 158%, and 537 MB, and makes speech 10.9 times faster than it plays, so it never falls behind. Seven Piper voices shipped (Kokoro did not): the full-precision models, 64 MB each and 110 MB for the two HD ones, because the half-size ones crash the engine on phones. They are off until you choose them, with a warning first and Remove all in Settings.

1.0 Voices

Dialogue in a second voice: who gets which?

Level 1: narrator and dialogue (recommended)

Anything inside quotation marks (“ ”, " ", « », 「 」) is dialogue; everything else is narration. You choose two voices per book. It's reliable because it never guesses who is speaking.

“Run,” she said, “now.” becomes three pieces: dialogue · narration · dialogue. Each is made in its own voice and joined with no gap, and the word timings are shifted per piece.

Level 2: a voice per character (experimental, later)

This needs to know who is speaking. The clues are a speech tag next to the quote (said Zorian, Zorian asked), names in the sentence before, and speakers taking turns in an untagged back-and-forth. On web novels that might get two lines in three right, and a wrong voice on a line is jarring. You'd assign voices to names in a list (Zorian → Voice 3). Only with a clear experimental label, if at all.

A third way, an on-device language model that tags speakers, is too heavy for a phone app today.
